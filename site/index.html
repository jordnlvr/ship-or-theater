Is it shipping, or is it theater?

Saw an AI claim that sounds too good to be true?

Every day your feed says AI just replaced a team, passed the bar exam, or built an app over lunch. Some of it is real. A lot of it is a polished demo, a cherry-picked number, or plain hype. Paste the claim in. Ship or Theater asks the six questions a skeptical engineer would ask and gives you a straight answer, with reasons: it ships, it’s mixed, or it’s theater.

How a claim gets judged

Six skeptics challenge the claim, a steelman makes its strongest honest case, and a judge weighs both sides. The result: a score from 0 to 100, or inconclusive when the evidence can’t support a number.

  1. 1

    Claim

    The text, plus optional context and a source link.

  2. 2

    Extractor

    Pulls out the specific assertions that can be tested.

  3. 3

    Six skeptics, in parallel

    One per dimension, each hunting for theater.

    • Reproducibility
    • Evidence quality
    • Scope honesty
    • Production-readiness
    • Novelty
    • Hidden caveats

    Steelman argues the strongest honest case for the claim.

  4. 4

    Judge

    Weighs skeptics against the steelman. Scores each dimension, or marks it inconclusive.

  5. 5

    Scorecard

    Weighted score, verdict, both sides of the argument. Terminal, Markdown or JSON.

If a skeptic fails, its dimension becomes inconclusive and drops out of the score. The run finishes; it never fills the gap with a guess.

The rubric is the point of view

Fixed, weighted and published. Each dimension scores 0 (pure theater) to 100 (real). The reality score is the weighted mean of the dimensions that had usable evidence.

AlphaFold 2 at CASP14

Google DeepMind · claimed Nov 30, 2020

4070
Show the full scorecardHide the full scorecard

“our latest AlphaFold system achieves a median score of 92.4 GDT overall across all targets.”

The claim states that DeepMind's latest AlphaFold system (AlphaFold 2 at CASP14) achieved a median GDT score of 92.4 across all targets in a blind structure-prediction assessment where experimentally determined structures were withheld from participants. The implied framing is that this near-experimental accuracy represents a breakthrough on the protein folding problem. The surrounding context adds that the code and weights were later publicly released, that ColabFold reportedly matched quality on CASP14 targets with a different sequence-search pipeline, and that the AlphaFold repository cautions that some CASP14 targets involved manual interventions and that reproducing competition results requires the original database versions.

  1. Reproducibility 20% 6870% confident

    CASP14 was a blind, prospective test with published targets and independent scoring. DeepMind released code and parameters, and ColabFold independently reported matching quality. Against that, the repository says some targets involved manual intervention and that exact competition results need the original database versions. So the 92.4 figure cannot be reproduced bit for bit, and the blog gives no methodology. The capability is reproducible in spirit, but the exact number is only partly so.

  2. Evidence quality 20% 7875% confident

    CASP14 is a community-run blind benchmark with independent assessors, which makes it much stronger evidence than a self-chosen demo. The weaknesses are that the cited source is a vendor blog, the median hides the distribution, 'all targets' is ambiguous (targets versus domains), and no confidence intervals are given. ColabFold's corroboration is real but is not an exact reproduction. The core number is well supported, and the surrounding framing is thinner.

  3. Scope honesty 20% 5565% confident

    The literal claim is tied to a named benchmark and metric, which is honest. But 'overall across all targets' sits in a blog titled 'a solution to a 50-year-old grand challenge', which invites a broader reading. CASP14 is a small set of mostly single-chain domains. GDT_TS says nothing about side chains, complexes, disorder or dynamics. The median hides tail failures, and the manual interventions on some targets are not mentioned in the claim. The number is honest and the surrounding generalization is overstated.

  4. Production-readiness 15% 6255% confident

    The claim is only an accuracy benchmark and gives no latency, cost, reliability or governance data. Still, the code and weights were released and are widely used, and ColabFold gives a faster search pipeline. That shows real deployability for the technology. The competition result itself involved some manual steps and specific database versions, so it is not evidence of a hands-off production pipeline. Compute and MSA search costs go unstated.

  5. Novelty 10% 8880% confident

    AlphaFold 2's CASP14 result was a widely recognized step change over prior methods, including AlphaFold 1, with a large gap to the next-best entrant. It is not a wrapper or rebrand. ColabFold reuses the model with a different search front end, so it does not undercut the novelty. The claim text does not itself say what is new, and 'latest' is now stale, which takes off a little.

  6. Hidden caveats 15% 5065% confident

    The claim leaves out several things: manual interventions on some targets, dependence on the original database versions, compute cost, and the median's hiding of low-scoring targets. It also omits that CASP14 targets are mostly single-chain domains with deep MSAs, and that 'latest' has lapsed. Some of these are disclosed in the AlphaFold repository, which helps. The headline number is real, but a reader of the claim alone would miss material caveats.

Strongest case for

The headline number is the best-documented part of the claim. DeepMind reported a median GDT_TS of 92.4 for AlphaFold 2 at CASP14, which is a blind, prospective assessment. The target structures were withheld from all participants until after predictions were submitted, and CASP's independent assessors scored them. That structure is much harder to game than a self-chosen benchmark, and it supports the 'across all targets' framing, since CASP scores the whole target set rather than a hand-picked subset. Post-competition developments also strengthen the case. DeepMind released AlphaFold 2 inference code and model parameters publicly, so independent parties can run the same model. The independent ColabFold team reported matching AlphaFold 2 prediction quality on CASP14 targets with a different sequence-search pipeline. That is evidence the capability is a property of the model and not of one tightly controlled setup. It was also a real step change. The context describes it as a blind-test result with independent follow-up, not a demo. The best case holds for the narrow claim: AlphaFold 2 scored a median GDT_TS of 92.4 on CASP14. It is weaker for the claims that this is the 'latest' system, that it was fully automated, that the exact result is reproducible, and that it generalizes to protein folding broadly.

Strongest case against

Hidden caveats (severity 58/100): The claim says 'across all targets' but omits that the CASP14 assessment and the AlphaFold repo itself note that some targets involved manual interventions, so the result was not purely automated end to end (contradicts a4).

Segment Anything's zero-shot transfer

Meta · claimed Apr 5, 2023

4070
Show the full scorecardHide the full scorecard

“The model is designed and trained to be promptable, so it can transfer zero-shot to new image distributions and tasks.”

The claim asserts that SAM, a model deliberately designed and trained to accept prompts, can generalize zero-shot to unseen image distributions and segmentation tasks without retraining. The supporting context cites the original paper (a promptable model plus a very large mask dataset), a public repository with code, checkpoints, notebooks and a permissive license, and an independent medical-imaging evaluation. That evaluation reports strong results against interactive baselines in single-point settings, but also large variation across tasks, a box-prompt advantage, and weak performance on some datasets. Together, these make the breadth of the zero-shot transfer claim the central thing to test.

  1. Reproducibility 20% 7270% confident

    Weights, inference code, notebooks and an Apache 2.0 license are public, and an independent group (Mazurowski et al.) actually ran SAM on 19 medical datasets, so the zero-shot behavior is independently checkable and not a cherry-picked demo. Training and the model-in-the-loop data engine are impractical to reproduce, and training code is not listed. Results also depend on prompt simulation choices. The 'new tasks' part has no independent replication in the context. Inference-level reproducibility is strong; full pipeline reproducibility is weak.

  2. Evidence quality 20% 6260% confident

    There is real evidence: a peer-reviewed paper with a broad zero-shot evaluation, and a third-party study on 19 medical datasets showing out-of-distribution wins on single-point prompts. The context supplies no numbers, named baselines or metrics, and most evidence is first-party. The independent evidence is mixed and covers one domain. It supports transfer to new image distributions better than transfer to new tasks, and it does not isolate promptability as the cause. Credible but thinly documented here.

  3. Scope honesty 20% 5060% confident

    The claim is worded as capability ('can transfer'), which fits the evidence loosely. But 'new tasks' reaches past promptable segmentation, which is what the independent evidence covers. The only independent out-of-distribution study shows large task variance, a dependence on prompt type, and poor results on some datasets. The context states these limits, which is more honest than the bare claim. The causal 'promptable, so transfers' link is asserted without an ablation separating it from data scale. Generalization is real but overstated by the unqualified phrasing.

  4. Production-readiness 15% Inconclusive

    The claim is about design and zero-shot capability, not deployment, and the context has no latency, cost, hardware or reliability data. Open checkpoints and a permissive license show experimental accessibility, not deployability. The independent study's task-to-task variation and poor results on some datasets suggest per-domain validation would be needed, but that is indirect evidence about a dimension the claim does not address, so no number is justified.

  5. Novelty 10% 7265% confident

    SAM is not a wrapper or rebrand. It combined a promptable segmentation task, a large model and a dataset of over a billion masks, a clear jump in scale and generality over earlier interactive segmentation methods. The prompt-based idea builds on prior interactive segmentation and borrows from NLP prompting, so part of the novelty is reframing and scaling. The independent medical study shows the zero-shot capability is real, though uneven. The context has no head-to-head comparison against earlier promptable models to isolate exactly what is new.

  6. Hidden caveats 15% 5055% confident

    The claim as phrased omits uneven transfer, the box-over-point prompt dependence, poor results on some datasets, compute needs, ambiguity and the missing semantic labels. The supplied context does surface the main performance caveats, and the paper discusses limitations, so this is incomplete disclosure and not concealment. Cost, latency and data bias or licensing go unmentioned. A middling score fits: caveats are findable but not in the claim.

Strongest case for

The claim is the central, well-documented thesis of a widely cited, peer-reviewed (ICCV 2023) paper, and it is backed by shipped, inspectable artifacts. The paper introduces a promptable segmentation model (point/box/mask prompts) together with the SA-1B dataset of over one billion masks on 11 million images. Training on that scale with a prompt-based objective is a coherent mechanism for zero-shot transfer, because the prompt supplies task specification at inference time rather than through fine-tuning. The public repo, with inference code, checkpoints, example notebooks and an Apache 2.0 license, lets anyone try to reproduce or extend the work. The independent Mazurowski et al. study did not just echo the authors. It tested SAM on 19 medical imaging datasets, a distribution far from natural images. It found SAM beat several interactive segmentation methods in almost all single-point settings, which is genuine independent evidence of out-of-distribution transfer. The same study reports honest limits: wide variation between tasks, box prompts beating point prompts, and poor results on some datasets. That pattern fits the claim as stated, which is 'can transfer', not 'transfers uniformly or perfectly'. The claim is also narrow: it concerns design intent and a capability for zero-shot transfer, and it does not promise clinical-grade or universal performance. Read that way, it is well supported. Caveat: this is the best case. Transfer is uneven, and the evidence here covers one independent domain (medical imaging) and mainly segmentation, so 'new tasks' is supported less than 'new image distributions'.

Strongest case against

Scope honesty (severity 58/100): The claim generalizes from 'designed to be promptable' to 'transfers zero-shot to new image distributions and tasks' with no qualifier on how far or how reliably. The 'tasks' scope is much broader than the evidence supports. The evidence covers promptable segmentation, not arbitrary new tasks.

  • Meta: Segment Anything inference code and trained model checkpoints (opens in a new tab)
  • Mazurowski et al.: Segment Anything Model for Medical Image Analysis, an Experimental Study (2023) (opens in a new tab)

Whisper's speech recognition release

OpenAI · claimed Sep 21, 2022

4070
Show the full scorecardHide the full scorecard

“We’ve trained and are open-sourcing a neural net called Whisper that approaches human level robustness and accuracy on English speech recognition.”

OpenAI claims to have trained and open-sourced Whisper, a neural network for speech recognition trained on 680,000 hours of multilingual audio, and asserts it approaches human-level robustness and accuracy on English speech recognition. The supporting context notes that models, inference code, and weights are released under the MIT license, that Whisper did not beat specialized models on LibriSpeech, that independent researchers found it robust to random noise but vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, and that another study found hallucinated content in about 1% of transcriptions, more often with long pauses. Overall, the claim combines a verifiable release/training-data statement with a broader, comparative capability claim (human-level robustness and accuracy) that depends on the benchmarks, conditions, and human baselines used.

  1. Reproducibility 20% 7270% confident

    The code and weights are MIT-licensed and public, and independent groups (Olivier and Raj, Koenecke et al.) have run their own evaluations, so this is not a cherry-picked demo. Training data is not released, and the human-parity comparison is hard to replicate from the provided context. WER also depends on the normalizer and decoding settings. Inference is reproducible, but the headline human-level comparison is only partly so.

  2. Evidence quality 20% 5060% confident

    The context confirms concrete facts: the 680k-hour training set, the open release, and the admitted LibriSpeech gap. It contains no human-baseline numbers or benchmark tables supporting 'approaches human level', so the core comparative claim rests on the vendor's own announcement. Independent studies show fragility (adversarial perturbation, about 1% hallucination) that WER-style metrics miss. They are narrow and don't directly refute the claim. Evidence exists but does not directly back the headline.

  3. Scope honesty 20% 4560% confident

    The wording 'approaches' is hedged, and OpenAI openly admits it did not beat specialized models on LibriSpeech, which counts in its favor. Even so, 'human level robustness and accuracy' is a broad framing. Independent work shows robustness holds for random noise but not adversarial perturbation, and hallucinations occur with long pauses, a failure mode humans don't show. Nothing in the context shows the claim generalizes to specialized domains, heavy accents or overlapping speech.

  4. Production-readiness 15% 5055% confident

    The open weights and MIT license make Whisper self-hostable and widely deployable, and about 99% of transcriptions in the Koenecke sample were free of hallucination. The announcement gives no latency, cost or throughput figures, and no confidence signals or governance guidance. Fabricated content in roughly 1% of transcriptions, concentrated in pauses, and adversarial susceptibility are real concerns for high-stakes use. It is usable with guardrails, but it is a research release and not a production service.

  5. Novelty 10% 5860% confident

    Whisper is a real, self-trained model and not a wrapper or rebrand. Its advance comes from weakly supervised training at scale (680k hours) and from combining multilingual ASR, translation and language ID in one zero-shot-robust model, not from a new architecture. Prior systems such as wav2vec 2.0 and commercial ASR already performed well. Open release is a distribution choice, not a technical advance. This is a meaningful but incremental advance.

  6. Hidden caveats 15% 4260% confident

    The vendor volunteered the LibriSpeech gap, which counts in its favor. The announcement does not surface the hallucination rate (about 1%, higher with long pauses) or adversarial fragility, and those came from third parties. It also gives no cost or latency figures, no subgroup error rates, no definition of the human baseline, and no information on data provenance or training-data release. These omissions are material, though the claim is hedged and the measured hallucination rate is modest.

Strongest case for

Whisper is a real, shipped, publicly verifiable system, not a demo. The code and weights were released under the MIT license, so anyone can download them and run their own evaluations. That is the strongest form of reproducibility, and the independent studies in the context (Olivier and Raj, Koenecke et al.) exist only because of it. The claim is also worded carefully: 'approaches' human-level, not 'matches' or 'exceeds'. The framing is robustness, meaning zero-shot performance across diverse conditions, rather than a record on a single benchmark. OpenAI backs this up by conceding that Whisper did not beat specialized models on LibriSpeech. A vendor that volunteers a benchmark where it lost is not cherry-picking in the usual way. The training scale (680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio) is a concrete, checkable design choice that plausibly explains broader robustness than models fit to one clean corpus. The independent evidence is mixed but not damning for the core claim. Robustness to random noise is consistent with the robustness claim. A roughly 1% hallucination rate in the Koenecke sample means most transcriptions were accurate, and that rate is low enough for many practical uses. Adversarial perturbation is a threat model that human-level robustness claims do not normally cover, since humans are not tested against adversarial audio either. Overall, the best case is that this is a genuine, widely usable, open release whose headline is a defensible approximation within the English conditions OpenAI tested. The best case is much weaker for the generalization assertion (a9). Nothing in the provided evidence shows the claim holding beyond the reported benchmarks, and the hallucination findings, which are higher on speech with longer pauses, point the other way. The 'human-level' comparison also rests on OpenAI's own evaluations, with no independent confirmation in the context.

Strongest case against

Scope honesty (severity 62/100): Headline says 'human level robustness and accuracy' on English speech recognition, but the evidence is selected benchmarks. The gap between those benchmarks and general real-world audio is not quantified.

GPT-4's top-decile bar exam claim

OpenAI · claimed Mar 14, 2023

4070
Show the full scorecardHide the full scorecard

“it passes a simulated bar exam with a score around the top 10% of test takers”

The claim, made by OpenAI at the release of GPT-4's text capabilities, is that the model passes a simulated bar exam with a score in about the top 10% of human test takers. Context indicates this rests on a simulated Uniform Bar Examination score of about 297 reported by Katz et al., a paper with OpenAI assistance, which placed the score between roughly the 68th and 90th percentiles depending on the comparison. A later re-evaluation by Martínez replicated the multiple-choice result but disputed the essay grading and the comparison population, estimating about the 62nd percentile against first-time test takers. The claim therefore bundles a passing-score assertion, a percentile-rank assertion that depends on population choice, and an implicit generalization to legal competence. It also carries questions about vendor involvement and reproducibility.

  1. Reproducibility 20% 5060% confident

    The multiple-choice result was independently replicated by Martínez, which is real reproducibility evidence for the core exam ability. But the headline percentile depends on essay grading by the study authors (not official bar graders) and on the comparison population. The independent re-evaluation lands at about the 62nd percentile, well short of the 90th. Vendor involvement, a possibly pre-release model, no published exact prompts, and unruled-out contamination limit third-party reproduction of the 'top 10%' figure. Partial reproducibility of the pass, weak reproducibility of the percentile.

  2. Evidence quality 20% 5260% confident

    There is quantitative data: a published study with a ~297 simulated UBE score, a stated 68th–90th percentile range, and an independent replication of the MCQ portion. That is well above screenshots and vibes. But the cited headline is the top of the range, the supporting paper acknowledges OpenAI help, essay grading was non-official and contested, and the population choice moves the percentile a lot. Run variance and contamination are undocumented. The evidence supports 'passes and lands above the median' much better than 'top 10%'.

  3. Scope honesty 20% 3865% confident

    The claim picks the most flattering end of a published range and drops the dependence on jurisdiction, administration and comparison population. The one independent estimate against first-time takers is ~62nd percentile, so the framing overstates standing. A simulated exam is also a narrow proxy that invites an implied leap to general legal competence, with no evidence on real legal tasks. The underlying capability is real, which keeps this above theater, but the headline is selectively framed.

  4. Production-readiness 15% 3550% confident

    The claim is a capability benchmark, not a deployment claim, so low production evidence is partly expected. Even so, there is no data on cost, latency, run-to-run variance, failure modes such as hallucinated citations, or governance for legal use. Nothing ties the tested configuration to the deployed ChatGPT/API model. Public availability shows access, not readiness. A deployment decision could not responsibly rest on the 'top 10%' headline.

  5. Novelty 10% 6860% confident

    GPT-4 clearly outperformed GPT-3.5 on this task, and the MCQ result replicated. This is a real capability improvement, not a wrapper or rebrand. The size is inflated by the percentile framing, and the likely cause is scaling gains rather than a new technique. Contamination was not ruled out, and no architecture or training detail was disclosed, so attributing the advance is hard. Moderate-to-good credit for a genuine but overstated step up.

  6. Hidden caveats 15% 4060% confident

    The headline omits the 68th–90th range, the population effect that yields ~62nd against first-time takers, author-graded essays, OpenAI's role in the study, possible contamination, run variance and the tested-versus-deployed model question. It also says nothing about failure modes such as hallucinated citations. Some of these appear in the published paper, and there is no fabrication, since the underlying score is partly replicated. Still, the omissions are material and consistently make the claim look better.

Strongest case for

This is one of the better-documented capability claims in the GPT-4 launch. The headline is backed by a named, peer-reviewed-style external study (Katz et al.) that reports a concrete simulated UBE score of about 297, above the passing cutoffs they considered. The multiple-choice portion is objective and was independently replicated by Martínez, a skeptic who set out to challenge the result. Even that critical re-evaluation puts GPT-4 at about the 62nd percentile against first-time takers, which is still a passing score and a clear step up from GPT-3.5's reported bottom-10% result. The 'top 10%' figure is the optimistic end of a published 68th–90th percentile range. So the real finding is 'passes, and lands well above the median'. The model was also released to the public through ChatGPT and an API waitlist, so users could try the capability themselves.

Strongest case against

Scope honesty (severity 68/100): Headline 'top 10%' is the most favorable end of a 68th–90th percentile range; the claim omits the range and the dependence on jurisdiction and administration.

Galactica's scientific knowledge claim

Meta · claimed Nov 16, 2022

4070
Show the full scorecardHide the full scorecard

“In this paper we introduce Galactica: a large language model that can store, combine and reason about scientific knowledge.”

The paper claims that Galactica, a large language model trained on scientific material, can store, combine and reason about scientific knowledge. The framing is broad and general-purpose across science. The context says the paper reports benchmark results on scientific question answering, mathematics and other tasks. It also says Meta released a public demo that generated literature reviews and scientific text. Contemporaneous reporting says the demo produced factual inaccuracies and fabricated scientific material and was taken offline within days. The model weights and loading instructions remain publicly available in five sizes. Overall, the claim joins a headline capability statement to benchmark evidence and a demo that reportedly failed on factual reliability.

  1. Reproducibility 20% 5555% confident

    Open weights in five sizes with loading instructions allow independent inference and benchmark re-testing, which is a real positive. Against that, the headline claim ('store, combine and reason') has no single reproducible test, the hosted demo was withdrawn, and training-level reproduction is not shown to be feasible. No independent replication of the paper's numbers is cited. Partial reproducibility is credible, so the score is moderate.

  2. Evidence quality 20% 4250% confident

    The paper reports benchmarks on scientific QA and mathematics, which is better than anecdote. But the supplied context has no numbers, baselines or independent replication, so strength can't be judged. The Ars Technica report of fabricated content and the quick takedown cut directly against 'store' and 'combine'. Benchmark results and open-ended behavior diverged, which indicates weak evidence-to-claim alignment. The score sits below the midpoint, not at the floor, because real benchmarks exist.

  3. Scope honesty 20% 3855% confident

    The claim is worded as broad scientific competence with no domains or limits stated. The open-ended 'combine' use case (literature reviews, wiki articles) failed in public, which contradicts the generalization the wording implies. In the paper's favor, it is framed as introducing a research model, it is bounded to 'scientific knowledge', and it reports benchmarks. The missing per-domain breakdown and limitations keep the score low-to-moderate.

  4. Production-readiness 15% 1570% confident

    The only real deployment attempt, the public demo, was pulled within days after producing fabricated and inaccurate scientific text. The context shows no guardrails, governance, cost or latency figures, or reliability metrics. Open weights are access, not deployability. The paper is a research contribution and does not strictly claim production readiness, which keeps the score above zero.

  5. Novelty 10% 4850% confident

    Galactica is a real but incremental contribution. It uses a standard Transformer, with novelty mainly in the curated scientific corpus, special tokenization for citations, reasoning and sequences, and open release. Domain scientific LLMs and Minerva existed already, and general LLMs already showed these capabilities. It is not a pure rebrand, but the context shows no isolated new capability, so the score is mid-range.

  6. Hidden caveats 15% 3060% confident

    The one-line framing omits the central failure mode: confident hallucination and fabricated citations, shown by the demo. It also gives no error rates, domain limits, cost or latency, or contamination caveats. Some credit is due because the full paper likely has a limitations section, which was not verified, and because the model remains accessible and testable. The well-evidenced gap between implied reliability and observed behavior keeps the score low.

Strongest case for

The best defensible case is that Galactica is real, documented research with released artifacts, and that its core claim is a modest, research-level one that the evidence partly supports. It is not a claim of a deployable, trustworthy scientific assistant, and that part fails. The paper is a primary source, submitted November 16, 2022. It reports training on scientific material and results on scientific QA, mathematics and other tasks. The authors' repository still gives model-loading instructions and lists five model sizes. So 'introduce an LLM' (a1) and 'independently accessible' (a8) are well supported. 'Store, combine and reason' reads as a capability claim about a research model, and benchmark results are the right kind of evidence for it. The scope is also bounded: it says 'scientific knowledge', not general intelligence. The main weakness is that the public demo did not hold up. Ars Technica reported factual inaccuracies and fabricated scientific material, and the demo was pulled within days. That undercuts a7 and weakens the 'combine' claim as shown by literature reviews and wiki-style text. Even so, the demo is a stress test of open-ended generation, which is a harder setting than the benchmarks. Benchmark competence and open-ended hallucination are separable findings, and the context does not show the benchmark results to be false. Releasing a public demo was also a transparent test, and it exposed the model's limits quickly.

Strongest case against

# print the rubric, no setup needed

Honest by construction

A tool that judges AI claims has to hold itself to the same bar. These are the rules it is built on.

  • Inconclusive beats invented

    No usable evidence means no score for that dimension. Nothing usable at all means no verdict.

  • Adversarial on purpose

    Six skeptics argue against the claim and one steelman argues for it before anyone scores.

  • Validated at every hop

    Each agent must return JSON that passes a schema. One retry, then it fails loudly.

  • Fails soft, never fakes

    A failed skeptic drops its dimension and the weights renormalise. The run still finishes.

  • A rubric you can argue with

    Six dimensions and their weights are fixed and published, so every score can be checked by hand.

  • Every run is signed

    Scorecards record the model and the time they were made. Re-run a claim when new evidence lands.
